Reflectance — how much, rather than which way

The two previous laws say where the light goes and nothing about how it divides. Fresnel gives the shares, and they depend on polarisation — so there are two equations and an average for ordinary light. Reflectance inherits Refraction, so one object now holds the whole interface: ask it for the refracted angle and it uses Snell, then ask it for a share and it uses that answer. The solver picks whichever equation the question needs.

    The two outgoing rays share the incident ray's width exactly, so four percent looks like four
    percent. Two well-known facts fall out of these equations rather than being stated anywhere:
    reflection reaches 1 at the critical angle, which is what the total in total internal
    reflection means, and the p-polarised share reaches 0 at Brewster's angle — 56.31° for glass —
    which is why polarised sunglasses cut glare off water.
  

  

    Approaching the critical angle the collapse is steeper than it looks. Set μ₁ 1.50 and μ₂ 1.00
    and creep from 41.00° upward: 62% still refracts at 41.00°, 2% at 41.81°, and nothing at all a
    hundredth of a degree later. cos(rr) is going to zero as the refracted ray swings
    flat along the surface, and it takes the whole transition with it.
