Total internal reflection — a law that only sometimes holds

      

        Chapter 1.4 ran out of answers past the critical angle: every Fresnel equation needs a
        refracted angle and there is not one. This chapter states what happens instead —
        r == 1 — and states it conditionally, so the solver reaches for it
        only in the regime where it is true. One equation, one when:, and the word
        total stops being a claim in prose.

        Drag the dividers to resize the panes; double-click one to put it back. Tick
        auto-run and the laws re-evaluate when you stop typing, and the page boots Ruby
        by itself next time. Both settings are remembered between visits.
      

      

        Anything you change is marked the way an editor marks it: a bar in the gutter beside every
        line you touched — green for new, blue for altered — a red wedge where lines were taken
        out, a dot on the tab and an M in the tree. Revert edits puts

        Open μ₁ above μ₂ and drag the angle past the critical value. Watch solved from
        change: below it the answer comes from :unpolarised, the Fresnel mean; above
        it from :everything_reflects. Nothing chooses between them by hand. The solver
        skips any equation whose guard is not satisfied, and prefers a guarded one when both apply,
        because a special case is the one that means something.
      

      

        The condition itself is not declared here. It belongs to refraction — it is the statement
        that Snell has no solution — so this chapter names it rather than restating it. That is
        what include Reflectance is for: the conditions, equations and quantities of
        every law underneath arrive with it.
      

      

        Fresnel gets there on its own, very nearly. At the critical angle itself the reflected
        share is 0.99999, and the ray is not trapped yet. A hundredth of a degree later there is no
        refracted angle to compute with, and the answer is exactly 1 because a law says so. Try
        surface_at(41.8103).solve(:r) against
        surface_at(41.82).solve(:r).
      

      

        This is what keeps light inside an optical fibre. The glass is denser than what surrounds
        it, the ray meets the wall at a shallow angle, and there is nowhere for it to go.
